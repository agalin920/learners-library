How to Use This Guide

This is a repository, not a manual. Most subjects are a ranked shelf: find the subject, pick a resource at the tier and in the dimension that fits what you need, and tick the box once you've worked through it. Everything is sorted best-first within tier.

Within every topic, rows run in three lanes: Courses (the main course first — badged Main — then alternatives), Books & readings ranked best-first, then Extras (narratives and podcasts) to enjoy on the side. Use the Hide textbooks and Hide extras filters to strip a lane out.

One rule everywhere: within each lane, rows are ranked best-first — start at the top and stop when you have what you need. Where subject-matter order matters (pre-calculus before calculus, fundamentals before theory), the topic groups themselves are arranged in learning order — work the groups top to bottom.

Dimensions

Textbook · Narrative · Course · Video · Interactive · Website · Article · Podcast · Problems/Drill · Reference · Primary · Survey · Code — the kind of resource, so you can pick the mode that fits how you want to learn a topic (e.g. a narrative to fall in love with a field, a textbook to master it, problems to prove it).

Editorial Charter — what this guide is & how it decides

This charter records what the guide is for and how it makes decisions, so its standards stay consistent as it grows. Read it before adding, cutting, or re-ranking anything.

Purpose

A curated best-of, not an index. The guide answers “what is the most effective way to learn X?” It is a ranked repository of the finest resources per subject — deliberately selective. Completeness is never the goal; quality and pedagogy are. If a resource isn’t among the best-taught or most illuminating for its topic, it doesn’t belong, however famous or free it is.

Learn smarter, not harder. Favor resources that build intuition, teach by doing, and are genuinely beloved for how well they teach — over exhaustive, rigor-for-its-own-sake, or merely canonical texts. All formats are welcome and equal in principle: books, courses, video, interactive sites, blogs, podcasts. Pick the format that teaches a given topic best.

Breadth of a whole education. The scope is the sciences, mathematics, and computing; the humanities — philosophy, history, literature; the practical crafts of building; and the transferable arts of learning, clear thinking, and judgment. The unifying test isn’t the subject but the aim: does this help someone understand the world more deeply or think more clearly? Wisdom and competence count alongside raw knowledge — which is why great lives, decision science, and a curiosity layer earn their place.

What it values

Pedagogy above prestige. The best teacher of a topic beats the most authoritative reference. A gentle, vivid, intuition-first resource outranks a comprehensive but forbidding one.

Quality is the only ranking axis — cost is not. Resources are judged purely on how well they teach. Whether a resource is free or paid is deliberately not tracked anywhere in this guide: cost never affects inclusion, ranking, or a resource's rationale.

Every format, deployed where it teaches best. A narrative to fall in love with a field; a textbook to master it; problems to prove it; a course or video for a guided path; an interactive site to explore. The dimension tag exists so a learner can pick the mode that fits their need.

A curiosity layer, on purpose. Beloved popular-science and “fall in love with the subject” books earn a place (the Curiosity Shelf and narrative picks) — momentum and wonder are part of learning, not a distraction from it.

What it avoids

No fluff. Exclude self-help, productivity-hacking, habits/lifestyle, business, leadership, and career books — even when a source everyone respects recommends them. This is a guide to learning subjects, not to life optimization.

No rigor-for-its-own-sake. Graduate reference texts prized mainly for exhaustiveness or austerity (the classic “impressive but forbidding” canon) are deliberately left out unless they are also genuinely well-taught. Depth is welcome; hazing is not.

No trivia-as-entertainment. The test for the Curiosity Shelf is understanding, not amazement. A popular book earns a place when it builds a real mental model of a subject (e.g. A Short History of Nearly Everything) — not merely because it is astonishing, bizarre, or fun at parties. Decline “fascinating facts,” “greatest stories never told,” bathroom-reader, and myth-debunking-for-amusement books: astonishment is not pedagogy, and disconnected trivia teaches no transferable model. The same bar demotes biography-as-trivia — a life earns inclusion only for how a mind worked and the judgment it teaches, never for anecdote. When a candidate is entertaining, ask what the reader can afterward reason about that they couldn’t before; if the honest answer is “nothing, but they know more strange facts,” it belongs on a gift list, not in this guide.

No dated dogma; no lateral duplicates. Skip resources that are influential but now superseded or contested, unless kept deliberately for literacy and clearly marked as such (and demoted). Don’t add a second resource that merely duplicates one already covered — add only if it teaches meaningfully better or fills a real gap.

How it’s ranked & structured

Three tiers, best-first. Essential (own the subject), Recommended (deepen or complement), Optional (reference or elite-tier). Within every group, resources are ordered best-first by pedagogical value. Tier encodes quality — so a lower-quality-but-worthwhile resource simply sits lower, rather than being excluded.

Grouped by topic for scanning. Larger subjects are split into topic clusters (tinted sub-headers) so a learner can find the best resource for a sub-topic at a glance. Small, single-theme subjects stay flat. Each resource carries a one-line “why” that states its teaching merit.

Cross-list only when genuinely dual-purpose. A resource may appear in two sections when it truly serves both (e.g. a systems text that anchors both CS and system design). Otherwise, one home, chosen by primary discipline.

How to develop it (method)

Read the primary source, not a summary. When mining a guide, syllabus, article, or repo, work from the actual source — summaries and research digests reliably capture the technical spine but drop the accessible / popular-book / curiosity layers, and can’t catch sources that changed since. This has burned this guide repeatedly. For one navigable authoritative source, go straight to it. For topics scattered across the web, research first to discover, then verify the shortlist against the real sources before adding. If a page is blocked or won’t load, say so plainly and get the real text — never reconstruct a list from memory and present it as read.

A source recommending a book is not the bar — fit is. Distinguished sources (a famous list, a respected blog, a great mathematician) make a candidate worth checking, not worth importing wholesale. A pull-quote is not an endorsement. Judge every candidate against this guide’s own purpose, which is often a different audience than the source had in mind.

Apply the charter as a filter, not a funnel. For every candidate, ask: is this among the best-taught or most illuminating for its topic, and does it fit a lane the guide serves? Most well-known resources that clear a low bar should still be declined — curation means saying no far more than yes. Self-help, productivity, business, and pop-persuasion are declined even when a respected source recommends them.

Rank down rather than exclude; flag when a companion is needed. A solid-but-reference-grade or lower-quality resource can be documented at a lower tier rather than dropped — tier carries the honesty. For a landmark work that is important but genuinely impenetrable (e.g. Hegel), include it with a “why” that says so and points to a companion/course — the entry then doubles as a reminder to find the best explainer. Never let an entry imply a forbidding primary text is a good on-ramp.

Verify before shipping. After any change, confirm the data parses, no resource is orphaned from its group, counts are intact, and the intended entries actually render. A correct earlier decision shouldn’t be quietly reversed by a later edit.
